\title{Reproducibility Failures in Quantized Model Evaluation:\\
Item Ordering Moves 4-Bit Accuracy and Leaves \texttt{bf16} Exactly Unchanged}

\author{Anonymous ARR submission}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
When a paper reports that 4-bit quantization costs two points of accuracy, that number
was not measured. It is one score minus another, so any part of the evaluation setup
that happens to penalise the two models unequally ends up inside the reported gap. We
evaluate
three instruction-tuned models at five precisions on reasoning benchmarks,
holding the engine, decoding configuration and prompts fixed, and vary properties
of the evaluation apparatus that no protocol we are aware of reports. Submitting
the same items in a different order changes 4-bit accuracy by up to
\hEightWorstSDTen{} points of standard deviation across \hEightSDOrderings{}
orderings while leaving \texttt{bf16} accuracy \emph{exactly} unchanged;
disabling the quantized matrix-multiply kernels drives the variation to zero, so
the mechanism is established by intervention. Generation is bitwise reproducible
\emph{within} a process, with zero differing items across 42{,}380 paired
comparisons, and is \emph{not} reproducible across processes, where the same
configuration diverges on up to 45\% of items; the common practice of repeating a
measurement in one process therefore cannot detect it. Under a fixed generation
budget, precision settings differ by an order of magnitude in how many tokens of
their output are discarded before scoring. We further show that how strictly an answer is extracted from the model's output
changes the apparent size of quantization damage, and changes it by different
amounts on different tasks and methods, so no single correction restores
comparability. Two of the three factors are nevertheless controllable, and we give
the remedies with their measured costs: generating all compared precisions inside one
process removes the cross-process variation at no cost, and disabling the quantized
matrix-multiply kernels removes the ordering variation for GPTQ and AWQ at roughly
$3.7\times$ evaluation wall clock. It does nothing for bitsandbytes NF4, which never used
those kernels and whose ordering sensitivity therefore has a separate cause that we
localise but do not identify. The third factor, the extraction rule, is a choice rather than
a nuisance parameter and must therefore be reported rather than standardised away. We
release 105{,}950 generations with per-item termination and length metadata, a runnable
order-invariance probe, and an audit of our own extraction pipeline, which had silently
degraded 2\% of that corpus until a census found it.
\end{abstract}

When a paper reports that 4-bit quantization costs two points of accuracy, that number
was not measured. It is the difference between two measurements: the model scored at
full precision, and the model scored after quantization. Anything about the evaluation
that penalises those two runs by the same amount cancels when you subtract. Anything
that penalises one more than the other does not, and lands in the reported gap instead.
This paper asks what those unequal factors are, and finds three that no evaluation
protocol we are aware of reports.

The framing matters because part of the answer is already known. \citet{kurtic2025bf16}
showed that reported degradation had been overstated and attributed the excess to
untuned quantization-side hyperparameters. We treat that as established and prior.
Our concern is the residual that survives quantization-side tuning, and our
mechanisms are orthogonal to theirs: they act on the evaluation, not on the
quantized model.

\paragraph{Changing every score is not the same as changing the gap between scores.}
That distinction organises the paper. It is well known that lenient answer
extraction raises absolute scores \citep{jo2025finding,yu2025xfinder}, and that
prompt formatting moves them substantially \citep{sclar2024formatspread}. What has
not been established is whether such choices change the \emph{gap} between a
full-precision model and a quantized one, which is the number papers actually report. A choice that lowers
every model's score by the same amount is a nuisance and nothing more, because the gap
between them survives it. A choice that lowers some models' scores more than others
changes that gap, and so contaminates every published comparison that did not report
the choice. We test for the second kind, and find it.

\paragraph{Three protocol factors, one of which we can turn off.}
Serving the same items through the same engine and varying only their submission
order changes 4-bit accuracy by up to \hEightWorstSDTen{} points of standard
deviation across \hEightSDOrderings{} orderings, while leaving \texttt{bf16}
accuracy \emph{exactly} unchanged under both extraction rules. Disabling the quantized
matrix-multiply kernels drives that variation to zero on \hEightPinned{} of the
quantized settings, so
the mechanism is established by intervention rather than inferred. Separately,
generation is bitwise reproducible \emph{within} a job and is not reproducible
\emph{across} jobs, which means the standard remedy of repeating a measurement in
one process cannot detect it. And under a fixed generation budget, settings whose
models write longer reasoning lose their answers to the cutoff more often, which turns
a difference in verbosity into what looks like a difference in ability.

\paragraph{The effect reaches the reported quantity, and not uniformly.}
How strictly the answer is extracted from the output interacts with precision, and
that interaction is \emph{uneven}: it differs across tasks and across quantization
methods,
both established by pre-registered contrasts rather than observed. The
consequence is the practical one. If the interaction were uniform, a single
correction would remove it. Because it is not, no protocol-level adjustment
recovers a comparable number, and the only remedy is to report the choices.

\paragraph{This is not an argument for scoring more permissively.}
Two facts prevent it. \citet{ren2026shortcut} find that 8.2--44.1\% of answers
graded correct are reached by shortcut hacking, so leniency over-credits. And we
measure that lenient extraction also \emph{destroys} correct answers, at a small
but non-zero rate, so it is not a monotone relaxation of strict extraction but a
different selection rule with two-sided error. The contribution is to
\emph{calibrate} extraction, not to loosen it.

\paragraph{The literature's centre of gravity runs against us, and that belongs here.}
The dominant finding in this area is that benchmarks \emph{understate}
quantization damage: \citet{marchisio2024multilingual} report a 1.7\% automatic
drop in Japanese corresponding to 16.0\% by human judgement, and
\citet{dutta2024accuracy} show that models matched on accuracy still diverge
behaviourally. We regard both directions as simultaneously true, because they are
different quantities. An accuracy delta can over-penalise formatting
\emph{and} under-detect behavioural change. Nothing here licenses a conclusion
that quantization is benign; the claim is about the comparability of reported
numbers, not about deployment risk.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
(i) Item ordering moves 4-bit accuracy and leaves \texttt{bf16} exactly
unchanged, with the mechanism pinned to a named kernel by intervention, and four gaps
identified in the engine's batch-invariance mode, the setting whose purpose is to make
results independent of how requests are grouped.
(ii) Generation is reproducible within a job and not across jobs, so
reproducibility must be measured across job boundaries; we report four candidate
mechanisms we refuted and the measurements that refuted them.
(iii) The precision-by-scoring interaction is real and heterogeneous across tasks
and methods, so no single correction recovers comparability.
(iv) Remedies with measured costs, rather than a diagnosis alone. Generating every
compared precision inside a single process removes the cross-process variation at no
cost; disabling the quantized matrix-multiply kernels removes the ordering variation for
GPTQ and AWQ at roughly $3.7\times$ evaluation wall clock, and does nothing for NF4,
whose mechanism is separate. Because a kernel flag is specific to one engine version, the
recommendation we actually make is the \emph{test} rather than the flag: verify
order-invariance for whichever kernels your backend selected, by running the same items
in several orderings and checking bitwise agreement. We release that probe.
(v) Three fields a protocol should report, each because we measured it moving the
quantized precisions and not the \texttt{bf16} baseline: the item submission order, whether
the compared precisions were generated in the same process, and the generation budget
together with its truncation rate.
(vi) A corpus of 105{,}950 generations with per-item termination and length
metadata, released so that any extractor can be re-evaluated without a GPU.
(vii) An audit of our own extraction pipeline, which had silently degraded 2\% of
that corpus until a census found it, offered as an instance of the effect we
measure rather than as an aside.

\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{A live contradiction about generation length.}
Whether low-bit quantization lengthens chain-of-thought output is currently
disputed. \citet{lian2026inflates} report that INT4/INT3 quantization can
preserve accuracy while \emph{increasing} reasoning-token usage, and propose a
CoT Token Inflation Ratio to quantify it; \citet{lotfi2026longer} likewise find
that aggressive post-training quantization lengthens CoT. \citet{liu2025quantization}
report the opposite: ``contrary to expectations, quantized models do not exhibit
increased output lengths.'' We take no position on which generator behaviour is
correct, because the disagreement has a consequence that neither side measures.
Under a \emph{fixed} generation cap, a model that emits longer traces is scored
wrong more often whenever its answer falls beyond the cap --- a scoring outcome,
not a competence one. No study on either side of this dispute sweeps the
generation cap, so no study can separate the two. That separation is our H1.

\paragraph{Degradation has already been partly explained --- by other causes.}
\citet{kurtic2025bf16} show that reported quantization degradation was
overstated, and attribute the excess to untuned quantization-side
hyperparameters. We treat that result as established and \emph{prior}, not as a
target: our claim concerns the residual that remains after quantization-side
tuning, and our mechanism is orthogonal to theirs. \citet{lotfi2026longer}
supply the most direct antecedent for the present work, finding that in as many
as 52\% of quantized-model failures the model reaches the correct answer in an
intermediate step but does not emit it as the final answer. Their interest is in
fixing the generator, via a logit penalty on overthinking markers; ours is in
what such failures do to a reported accuracy delta when they are left in place.

\paragraph{The centre of gravity runs the other way.}
The dominant finding in this literature is that benchmarks \emph{understate}
quantization damage, not overstate it. \citet{marchisio2024multilingual} find a
1.7\% automatic drop in Japanese corresponds to 16.0\% by human judgement, and
\citet{dutta2024accuracy} show that models matched on accuracy still diverge
behaviourally --- the ``flips'' framing, which is theirs and which we do not
claim. We regard both directions as simultaneously true: an accuracy delta can
over-penalise formatting \emph{and} under-detect behavioural change, because
these are different quantities. Our contribution is to the first, and it does
not license any conclusion about the second.

\paragraph{Calibrating extraction, not loosening it.}
An argument that evaluation should simply be more permissive is already
refuted. \citet{ren2026shortcut} find that 8.2--44.1\% of answers graded correct
are reached by shortcut hacking, so lenient extraction over-credits as surely as
strict extraction under-credits. We therefore treat the strict/permissive
contrast as a measurement instrument to be characterised rather than a knob to
be turned in one direction, and we report both extraction rules throughout.

\paragraph{Prompt- and protocol-sensitivity.}
\citet{hua2025flaw} ask whether prompt sensitivity is a genuine model property
or an artifact of evaluation, and we are structurally the sequel: they vary the
prompt, we vary the scorer, and both ask whether a reported effect survives the
instrument. Their null result on MATH deserves emphasis rather than a footnote
--- extraction there already uses symbolic equivalence, so a well-engineered
verifier leaves little for a protocol effect to explain. This bounds our claim
rather than supporting it, and it is why our task selection targets benchmarks
whose canonical extractors are regular expressions.
\paragraph{The nearest prior work, and what it deliberately designed out.}
\citet{pape2026silent} is the closest study to our \S\ref{sec:h8} and must be
distinguished precisely. They survey 35{,}000 publications, find that only 36\% of
papers without released code name even their inference backend, and show that
switching backend alone moves GSM8K by 16.6 points. We adopt their framing and
their survey rather than repeating it. But their controlled comparison runs at
\emph{batch size one}, chosen explicitly to prevent batching-induced instability,
holds precision at FP16, and varies only random seeds within an engine; batch
invariance and quantized kernel selection do not enter. The instability they
design out is the one we make the object of study: we hold the engine, the
weights and the decoding configuration fixed, vary only which items share a
batch, and attribute the resulting divergence to a named quantized kernel by
intervention. Their root-cause analysis independently supports our wider point ---
their two largest outliers were a spurious BOS injection and an unnoticed
repetition penalty, worth 8.3 and 11.7 points, both configuration defaults rather
than model properties.

\citet{sclar2024formatspread} establish that spurious formatting choices move
scores substantially, and \citet{jo2025finding} and \citet{yu2025xfinder} show
that answer extraction from reasoning traces is itself an unreliable step.
\citet{schaeffer2023mirage} provide the methodological template we follow: a
headline effect reattributed, in part, to the measurement instrument.
The lm-evaluation-harness \citep{biderman2024lessons} is our instrument, and its
canonical filters are the object of study rather than an implementation detail.

\paragraph{Serving configuration is a confound we pin, not an axis we explore.}
\citet{pape2026silent} find that inference-backend choice alone moves benchmark
scores by up to 16.6 points, and \citet{masoudian2026sideeffect} attribute
roughly 39\% of out-of-the-box variance to it. We read this as a reason to
freeze the engine, version, and every decoding parameter across all precision
settings, which
we do and report; a floating serving configuration would make precision effects
unidentifiable. We claim no novelty on this axis.

\paragraph{Adjacent evaluations.}
Broad compression benchmarks \citep{vonrad2026unicomp,wang2025slmquant} and
task-difficulty studies \citep{lee2025tradeoffs} establish the accuracy
results we work against; \citet{lee2025tradeoffs} in particular report that
quantization disrupts instruction-following, which is the strongest available
objection to our framing and which our compliance/competence split is designed
to address directly rather than deflect. \citet{prasad2026factorial} run a
factorial quantization analysis, so factorial design is not our contribution;
their outcome domain is safety alignment under sampling temperature, which is
disjoint from ours. \citet{kurt2026which} report the observation this paper
sets out to explain --- strict-versus-flexible GSM8K gaps of 41 to 64 points
across fourteen \texttt{llama.cpp} quantizations --- without interpreting the
gap; we reproduce their numbers as an external anchor.
\citet{oladri2026silent} and \citet{rasmussen2026calibration} report related
failure-mode and scoring-rule effects, the former over FP32/FP16/NF4 only and
so without bearing on GPTQ or AWQ.

\paragraph{What is new here.}
Permissive extraction is well known to raise absolute scores. What has not been
established is whether it changes the quantization \emph{difference}: an
artifact in a level need not be an artifact in a delta, and the two are
routinely conflated. We decompose the measured strict delta into competence,
compliance, and truncation components, pre-register the decomposition before any
generation exists, and report the residual precision effect that survives it.

\subsection{Experimental design}
\label{sec:design}
% 3 models x 5 precisions x tasks; one-factor-at-a-time from a fixed
% reference condition. Llama-3.1-8B access confirmed 2026-08-23, so the
% armed substitution to Qwen2.5-7B is VOID and is not mentioned as live.
We evaluate three instruction-tuned models spanning a 3--8B size gradient:
Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct, Phi-3.5-mini-instruct, and Llama-3.1-8B-Instruct. Each is
pinned by commit SHA rather than tag, since tags move and reproducibility holds
only within identical versions.

We compare five precision settings, which we call \emph{arms}: \texttt{bf16},
GPTQ-8bit, GPTQ-4bit, AWQ-4bit, and bitsandbytes NF4. Five rather than two, because
ordering them by bit width gives a graded series instead of a single before-and-after
comparison. If accuracy falls steadily from \texttt{bf16} through 8-bit to 4-bit,
that is much harder to explain as a measurement artifact, since an artifact of the
evaluation has no reason to track bit width in order.

All manipulations are one-factor-at-a-time from a single fixed reference
condition, so every reported effect is attributable to the one factor varied.
NF4 is included in the reference condition but excluded from the token-budget
sweep, and we state the reason rather than the exclusion alone: NF4 has no CUDA
kernels in our serving engine, and it is quantized \emph{in flight} at load
rather than loaded from a pre-made checkpoint. That is a backend difference
layered on the bit-width difference, which we name explicitly rather than absorb.

\subsection{Quantization and calibration}
\label{sec:quant}
% THE most likely reviewer attack surface.
This is the most likely attack surface on the paper, so we state it in full.
Both GPTQ and AWQ are produced by one tool, through one calibration code path,
at \emph{one} normalised budget: 512 sequences of at most 2048 tokens drawn from
a single pinned shuffle of \texttt{ultrachat\_200k}, identical for every arm,
every bit-width, and every model. We report the realised token count rather than
the product of those two numbers, since sequences shorter than the cap
contribute their own length.

Normalisation is necessary because the reference implementation's own examples
differ by \textbf{8$\times$} in calibration budget between the two methods
(GPTQ at 512$\times$2048 symmetric; AWQ at 256$\times$512 asymmetric). Accepting
those defaults would mean a GPTQ-versus-AWQ difference confounded with an
8$\times$ calibration difference \emph{and} a symmetry difference. We therefore
normalise AWQ to symmetric 4-bit weight-only quantization, which is a deliberate
deviation from its published example, and we flag it as such.

Group size is 128 and activation reordering is disabled for every GPTQ arm,
matching the vendor's own released checkpoints. Disabling reordering is set
explicitly rather than left to the library, whose default silently applies a
static reordering. Every parameter above is hashed into a configuration digest
carried on each generation record, so a reader can verify that no arm drifted.

All arms are self-quantized with identical settings. Vendor checkpoints are used
only as an external cross-check, never mixed into the main matrix, because a
widely used third-party GPTQ checkpoint for one of our models enables activation
reordering against the vendor's own setting --- exactly the kind of silent
confound this design exists to exclude.

\subsection{The scoring ladder}
\label{sec:ladder}
Every generation is stored once and scored many times. We apply a ladder of
extractors to the \emph{same} stored text, so the entire ladder costs zero
additional generation and every step sees byte-identical inputs. We call each step a
\emph{rung}, and refer to them by name rather than by number throughout:

\begin{enumerate}\itemsep0pt
  \item \textbf{strict} --- the evaluation harness's canonical regular
    expression, used verbatim.
  \item \textbf{strict-last} --- the same expression selecting the
    \emph{last} match instead of the first, which separates answer
    \emph{position} from extraction \emph{strictness}.
  \item \textbf{permissive} --- the harness's flexible extractor, with its rules
    enumerated in the pre-registration.
  \item \textbf{symbolic} --- equivalence checking rather than string matching.
  \item \textbf{judge} --- a trained answer verifier \citep{chen2025xverify}.
\end{enumerate}

\textbf{The headline contrast is strict versus permissive.} Both are deterministic,
auditable, and judge-independent, which is what makes the judge a robustness check
rather than a load-bearing dependency. That distinction is doing real work here rather
than being a precaution: we validated the strict and permissive rungs against human
labels (Appendix~\ref{app:rubric}), but the judge itself was never scored against them, so
we report it as an unvalidated extractor and rest no claim on it. The strict-last variant exists because a harness that takes the first match will
score a model that restates its answer differently from one that does not,
independent of correctness.

\paragraph{An audit of our own extractor, and what it found.}
Because this paper's claim is that unreported measurement choices move reported
numbers, we audited our own extraction rather than assuming it. Our permissive extractor applied a commitment-phrase rule
ahead of the harness's flexible-extract regex, and returned the committed answer
with its unit attached (\texttt{"5 days"} against a gold of \texttt{"5"}). Because
the harness's regex admits no alphabetic character, it could not have produced
those values: the defect was ours, ordered ahead of published practice, and it
returned worse answers than the harness would have on 2{,}068 of 105{,}950
generations. A full census of every rung-monotonicity violation is what found it;
our unit tests passed throughout, because the fixture set contained no generation
that continued past its committed answer. Two interaction estimates changed
conclusion when it was repaired and one changed sign. All numbers reported here are post-repair. We record the episode because it is an
instance of the effect this paper measures rather than an aside: a well-intentioned
extractor improvement silently degraded 2\% of a six-figure corpus, our unit tests
passed throughout, and only an exhaustive audit surfaced it. It is also why we report
a census of every affected item rather than a sampled estimate.

\subsection{Compliance, competence, and truncation}
\label{sec:decomp}
We decompose the measured strict delta into three additive components: a
\emph{competence} component (the model does not know the answer), a
\emph{compliance} component (it knows but does not emit the answer in the
expected form), and a \emph{truncation} component (it would have emitted the
answer but ran out of budget). Recovering the third requires two fields that
cannot be reconstructed after the fact --- the engine's termination reason and
the generated token count --- so both are recorded on every generation, and our
writer refuses any record missing either.

Compliance is treated as a dependent variable in its own right, not merely as a
correction term. We also distinguish an answer being \emph{extractable} from the
model having \emph{committed} to it: a fallback rule that returns the last number
in a truncated trace has extracted something the model never asserted, and
counting that as compliance would inflate the very component we are trying to
measure.

\subsection{Serving configuration and determinism}
\label{sec:determinism}
The serving configuration is frozen and identical across every arm: one engine,
one pinned version, one set of decoding parameters, greedy decoding throughout.
This is not incidental rigour. Backend choice alone moves benchmark scores by up
to 16.6 points \citep{pape2026silent} and accounts for roughly 39\% of
out-of-the-box variance \citep{masoudian2026sideeffect}, so a serving
configuration that floated across arms would make precision effects
unidentifiable regardless of sample size.

Greedy decoding is not by itself sufficient for reproducibility, because
inference engines batch requests and batched reductions are not
order-invariant. We therefore enable batch-invariant kernels, run with tensor
parallelism of one (batch invariance is broken above one), disable prefix
caching (it changes greedy output and is explicitly outside the invariance
guarantee, and it would otherwise help throughput considerably on our shared
few-shot prefixes), and fix the maximum number of co-resident sequences to a
value \emph{measured} rather than assumed: the largest setting that is bitwise
reproducible across repeated identical runs for \emph{every} precision arm, which
is a minimum over arms because a single shared value is required.

We report that value, the per-arm bitwise disagreement rates behind it, the GPU
model, and the CUDA and driver versions. Where a quantized arm is less
reproducible than \texttt{bf16}, the difference is a noise floor on every
contrast involving that arm, and we pre-commit to claiming no effect smaller
than it.

\subsection{Item ordering moves 4-bit accuracy and leaves \texttt{bf16} unchanged}
\label{sec:h8}

% ⚠️ EVERY NUMBER HERE COMES FROM paper/tables/h8_*.tex, generated by
% scripts/make_h8_tables.py from the job reports (R17.6.2). Do NOT inline a
% statistic: the 10-ordering job (R17.3) replaces all of them, and a hand-edited
% number nobody re-derives is exactly the failure mode this paper is about.

We serve every arm through a single engine version (\hEightEngine) with
batch-invariant kernels enabled, tensor parallelism disabled, prefix caching
off, and a fixed decoding configuration, then vary one parameter that no
evaluation protocol we are aware of reports: \emph{the order in which items are
submitted}. Because requests are batched dynamically, an item's position determines which other
items share its batch, and therefore the shapes over which the engine sums its
intermediate results. Floating-point addition is not associative, so summing the same
numbers in a different order can differ in the last bit, and in chain-of-thought
generation a single differing token changes every token after it.

Holding the ordering fixed and running the same items again changes nothing: not one
of the \hEightNCells{} model-and-precision combinations we measured produced a single
differing output. So every difference reported below is caused by the ordering, not by
run-to-run variation.

\paragraph{Aggregate accuracy moves, and only for quantized arms.}
Table~\ref{tab:h8-sd} gives the standard deviation of accuracy across
\hEightSDOrderings{} orderings of the same \hEightItems{} items at the served
batch size we adopt. For \texttt{bf16} the accuracy is \emph{identical} under all
\hEightSDOrderings{} orderings, at both scoring rungs: \hEightBFTenZero{} of
\hEightBFTenCells{} cells show exactly zero variation. Every quantized arm moves,
by up to \hEightWorstSDTen{} points of standard deviation.

\paragraph{Why we report a standard deviation and not a spread.}
A minimum--maximum spread is monotone in the number of orderings sampled, so it
grows with effort rather than converging. We can show this rather than assert it:
the same quantity measured over three orderings gave a spread of 1.6 points and
over \hEightSDOrderings{} gives \hEightRangeTen{}, with no change to the
underlying effect. Any spread reported over a handful of runs is therefore a lower
bound on the spread its own protocol would have produced with more. The standard
deviation, by contrast, stayed within a narrow band across arms and rungs.

Three properties matter more than the magnitude. The shift is
\textbf{deterministic} given an ordering rather than sampling noise, so it
cannot be averaged away by repeated runs. It is \textbf{bidirectional} ---
accuracy moves both up and down across orderings, so no ordering is the correct
one and the effect cannot be calibrated away. And it is \textbf{attributable by
intervention} rather than inferred, as follows.

\paragraph{The mechanism is a named kernel.}
Disabling Marlin and Machete through \texttt{VLLM\_DISABLED\_KERNELS}, changing
nothing else, drives order-induced divergence to zero on \hEightPinned{} arms
(Table~\ref{tab:h8-mechanism}) --- on generated text \emph{and} on accuracy.
These are the quantized matrix-multiply kernels vLLM selects by default, and
they are not covered by its batch-invariant mode: the AWQ kernel path carries a
batch-invariance guard, the GPTQ path carries none, and the sampler and kernel
autotuning paths are likewise unguarded. We report this as a defect in the
feature rather than in either kernel; AWQ has the guard and is nonetheless
affected, so the guard as implemented is insufficient.

\textbf{What this establishes, and what it does not.} The intervention locates the
mechanism in a specific pair of kernels and shows the effect is not merely correlated with
them, because removing them removes it. It does not explain the arithmetic: we do not
identify which reduction differs, in what order, or why AWQ's batch-invariance guard fails
to hold. We claim the localisation, which is what an intervention buys, and not the
explanation.

unresolved.\footnote{\texttt{bitsandbytes} is proposed for out-of-tree
maintenance in vLLM RFC~\#39583, on performance grounds. Two explanations are

concentrates at larger batch sizes, or a different kernel-selection path
entirely --- and we do not have evidence to separate them.}

\paragraph{The size of the effect depends on where you measure it.}
The level at which divergence is measured changes its apparent size by about
$\hEightTextCorrRatio\times$: generated text diverges on up to
\hEightWorstText\% of items, while scored correctness diverges on at most
\hEightWorstCorr\% at the same configuration, with the extracted answer between
them. Reporting text divergence alone would substantially overstate the impact on
any reported number, and reporting correctness alone would conceal how much of the
trace is unstable, so we report all three levels at every batch size we swept
(Appendix~\ref{app:h8frontier}).

Notably the permissive rung is the more order-sensitive of the two at the
answer level, because it accepts the last number anywhere in the trace and the
trace is what diverges, whereas strict extraction requires a committed marker
that is comparatively stable. Leniency therefore buys recovery of truncated
answers at the cost of exposure to batch-composition noise --- which is an
argument for calibrating extraction rather than loosening it
(\S\ref{sec:ladder}).

\subsection{Reproducibility holds within a process and fails across them}
\label{sec:crossjob}

% 🚨 MOVED HERE FROM DISCUSSION 2026-09-05, on the user's report that the paper was
% hard to follow. This is contribution (ii): the abstract foregrounds it and the
% contributions list it second, but it was first REPORTED in the Discussion, so a reader
% looking for it in Results could not find it. A headline contribution with no Results
% subsection is a structural hole, not a presentation preference.

The natural defence against measurement noise is to run it twice, and here that
defence fails in a specific and instructive way. Within a single process,
generation is bitwise reproducible: we observe zero differing items across
42{,}380 paired comparisons. Across process boundaries, the same configuration on
the same hardware diverges on up to 45\% of items for one model, and moves its
accuracy by over a point. So a practitioner who repeats a measurement in one
process obtains perfect agreement and concludes the pipeline is deterministic,
which is exactly the wrong conclusion, reached by exactly the check a careful
person would run. Reproducibility has to be assessed across job boundaries, and
we know of no protocol that does so.

\paragraph{The mechanism we did not find, and what we ruled out.}
We report the cross-process effect without a mechanism, and we state what was
eliminated so that the gap is bounded rather than open. Four candidates were
tested and refuted. Differing KV-cache block counts from per-invocation memory
profiling: refuted, because the engine-reported cache sizes are byte-identical
between jobs, cell for cell. Batching scheduled by memory rather than by
sequence count: refuted, because one arm is stable at a concurrency of
$12.36\times$ while another diverges at $10.70\times$, so the two are not
separable. Weight provenance, base repository against local checkpoint: refuted,
because it predicts one model exactly and is contradicted by the other two.
Sampling configuration, including a hidden repetition penalty read from each
model's own generation config: refuted, because the model shipping the most
aggressive defaults is the only one that is perfectly reproducible. What remains
untested is state that is compiled or cached once per process, which is
consistent with the within-process zero, and we identify it as the next
hypothesis rather than claiming it.

has no such remedy. The correction that would make GPTQ W4A16 comparable on BBH

not serve AWQ at the same bit width. So the practical recommendation is not a
better default but a disclosure requirement: the extraction rule belongs in the
reported protocol, because no post-hoc adjustment recovers it.

\paragraph{Where the interaction is absent, both cases are informative.}
GPTQ W8A16 is null on both generative tasks (\hThreeBbhGptqEight{}~pp,

\subsection{Even a constrained answer space does not protect the delta}
\label{sec:h5h6}

ARC-Challenge asks for one of four letters, so extraction should have nothing to distort.
We tested that directly. The same \hSixItems{} items and the same prompt were scored two

log-likelihood of the four letters after the prompt's answer prefix, which removes the
extraction step entirely. Weights, engine and hardware are identical between the two.

The registered quantity is the quantization delta under generation minus the same delta

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
% C7, W5.

\paragraph{What to do: two controllable factors, and one that must be reported.}
The three factors divide, and the division is principled rather than a matter of how
much effort we spent on each. Item ordering and the process boundary are \emph{nuisance
parameters}: they have no bearing on the question anyone is asking, so the right response
is to pin them, and we measured what that costs. Generating every compared precision
inside one process removes the cross-process variation entirely and costs nothing, which
follows from the exact zero over 42{,}380 paired comparisons in
\S\ref{sec:crossjob}. Disabling the quantized matrix-multiply kernels removes the
ordering variation for GPTQ and AWQ at roughly $3.7\times$ evaluation wall clock, which is
a cheap trade for a one-off benchmark run and would not be a sensible one for production
serving. It does nothing for NF4, whose ordering sensitivity survives the disable and
whose mechanism we did not identify.

The extraction rule is different in kind. It is not a nuisance parameter but a
\emph{choice} about what counts as a correct answer, and \S\ref{sec:h3} shows its effect
varies by quantization method and by task. A quantity that varies with the thing you are
measuring cannot be standardised away, so it has to be reported. That is why our
recommendation is asymmetric: pin the first two, report the third.

What none of this licenses is a rule of the form ``report accuracy to one decimal place''
or ``average over $k$ runs''. Averaging cannot help with a factor that is deterministic
given a configuration, and numerical precision was never the problem.

\paragraph{Recommend the test, not the flag.}
A kernel name and an environment variable are specific to one engine version, and ours
will age. The durable form of the same advice is a test: run the same items in several
orderings and check whether the outputs agree bitwise, for whichever kernels your backend
selected. That question stays meaningful when the kernel names change, and it is what we
release alongside the corpus. Our own use of it is the worked instance rather than the
recommendation itself.

\paragraph{Why the scoring rule must be reported rather than corrected.}
The heterogeneity in \S\ref{sec:h3} is why every recommendation here is a
disclosure requirement rather than an adjustment: the sign of the scoring effect
depends on which method and which task a paper used, so there is no single
correction to apply.

\paragraph{On auditing one's own instrument.}
The largest single error we found was our own. An extraction rule we had added
ahead of the harness's returned worse answers than the harness would have, on
2{,}068 of 105{,}950 generations, and our unit tests passed throughout because no
fixture exercised a generation that continued past its committed answer. Two of
our interaction estimates changed conclusion when it was repaired and one changed
sign. We report this because it is the same phenomenon as the rest of the paper
observed from the inside, and because it suggests that a census over every item is
worth more than a fixture set when the quantity of interest is a small
differential.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Three unreported properties of the evaluation apparatus move reported
quantization degradation: item submission order, the process boundary a
measurement is taken across, and the generation budget. Because the scoring
rung interacts with precision heterogeneously across tasks and methods, no
single correction recovers comparability, and the remedy is to report these
choices rather than adjust for them. We release the full generation corpus so
that any extraction rule can be re-evaluated without a GPU, including ours,
which auditing found to be defective.

\section*{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}
% Must include: no factual-recall task (TriviaQA cut; the structural control is
% the ARC mode pairing); L40S/SM89 single-hardware determinism claim;
% self-quantized-only main matrix; 3 models is a fixed effect not a population;
% judge is open-weight not human-level; annotation n and that annotators were
% unpaid co-authors.

\paragraph{The reproducibility claims are single-hardware and single-engine.}
Every determinism result holds for one GPU (L40S, SM 8.9), one engine version, and
one CUDA and driver pair, all of which we report. Batch invariance is
version-specific and kernel-specific, so neither the ordering result nor the
cross-job result should be read as a claim about other hardware. We also cannot
serve a stock AWQ checkpoint in \texttt{bfloat16} on this engine version at all,
which is itself a comparability limitation rather than only an inconvenience:
published AWQ numbers are not dtype-comparable to GPTQ unless dtype is reported.

\paragraph{The cross-job mechanism is undiagnosed.}
We establish that generation is bitwise reproducible within a job and not across
jobs, and we report four refuted hypotheses with the measurements that refuted
them. We do not identify the cause. The finding is therefore an observation about
what a third party will encounter, not an explanation, and our recommendation is
correspondingly procedural rather than a fix.

\paragraph{The competence decomposition rests largely on bounds.}
The always-compliant stratum falls below half the paired items in 14 of 27 cells,
and in the worst case (Qwen on the hash-marker GSM8K arm) it is three items. We
therefore report the point estimate and the Manski bounds together for every cell,
with the stratum size as a first-class number, rather than presenting a point
estimate whose subgroup is too small to support it. Wide bounds are reported as
wide. The decomposition is a supporting analysis in this paper, not a headline.

\paragraph{We do not report a counted survey of protocol reporting.}
Our pre-registration includes an audit computing, for each published degradation
figure we cite, the confidence interval implied by that paper's own evaluation
size. We verified four sources and stopped. Reporting four of fourteen would
invite the question of why those four, and the honest answer would be that they
were the four easiest to verify, which is not a selection rule a reader should
accept. We therefore make the general claim on \citet{pape2026silent}'s survey of
35{,}000 publications, which finds that only 36\% of papers without released code
name even their inference backend, and we cite our four verified cases as
instances rather than as a rate.

\paragraph{Two registered hypotheses are not reported, and we name them rather than omit them.}
Our pre-registration includes two hypotheses we do not report. H2, the rate at
which a correct answer appears in a generation's trace without reaching final
answer position, is implemented and its false-positive guard is registered, but it
is only measurable on records produced after a mid-study change to where the
terminator is applied, and we have not recomputed it. H5, the same
precision-by-scoring interaction under zero-shot prompting, has no instrument: our
generation stages are fixed to the few-shot variant. In both cases a partial
number would have been available and we judged that reporting one would be worse
than reporting none, because a half-measured hypothesis invites the reader to
weight it as though it were measured. They remain in the pre-registration, which
is the record that they were planned rather than added or dropped to suit a result.

\paragraph{Scope deliberately excluded, and what that costs.}
There is no factual-recall task: TriviaQA was cut, so every task here is a
reasoning task whose canonical extractor is a regular expression, and the
structural control is the ARC mode pairing rather than a second domain. Our main
matrix is self-quantized throughout, at a normalised calibration budget, which
isolates the recipe but means we do not speak to arbitrary published checkpoints.
Three models cannot support a variance estimate, so model is a fixed effect and no
claim generalises to a model population. Finally, our anchor comparison against
official checkpoints tests comparable quality at matched settings rather than
reproduction, because the official checkpoints were calibrated on data we do not
have; one of its three arms failed its pre-registered band, and we report the
failure and the grounds on which we proceeded.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
\label{sec:ethics}

This work evaluates existing open-weight models on existing public benchmarks
and introduces no new model, no new dataset of human origin, and no deployment.
We see three ethical considerations worth stating.

\paragraph{Licensing, and one model we can run but not redistribute.}
Every model and dataset is used within its licence. Two cases need naming
rather than assuming. Qwen2.5-3B-Instruct is released under the Qwen research
licence rather than Apache-2.0, and we report it as such. Our primary judge is
released under CC-BY-NC-ND-4.0, which permits us to run it and report numbers
but forbids redistributing the weights or any derivative, so the artifact
release contains our judging code and its outputs and not the model. We name a
second, Apache-2.0 judge for exactly this reason, so that the reliability
claim can be reproduced by someone unwilling to accept the first licence.

\paragraph{Human validation of the extraction rungs.}
We validated the automated extraction rungs against 250 human labels drawn from
\texttt{pilot-v2}, stratified over 48 cells of model, precision and task, with 120 items
labelled twice for agreement. Inter-annotator agreement was 0.975 raw (95\% CI 0.929 to
0.991), Cohen's $\kappa$ 0.943 and Gwet's AC1 0.968; we report AC1 as primary because the
label distribution is skewed and $\kappa$'s chance term penalises exactly that imbalance.
The three disagreements were resolved by applying the written rubric as a rule lookup, with
the governing clause recorded per item, and one of them resolved to a label neither
annotator had chosen. Blinding was by omission rather than concealment: the precision
condition, the parser's verdict and the judge's verdict are absent from the annotation file,
item order was shuffled independently per annotator with published seeds, and no label was
ever pre-filled. The rubric, the seeds, the labels and the adjudication record are released.

The LLM judge rung remains \emph{unvalidated} and we state that rather than imply
otherwise: we report no agreement statistic for it and no claim in this paper rests on it.
The headline contrast is strict against permissive, both deterministic and auditable.

\paragraph{What a paper about measurement error could be misused to argue.}
Our findings show that reported quantization degradation is in part an artifact
of evaluation protocol. That could be read as licence to dismiss reported
degradation generally, and we do not support that reading. The dominant finding
in this literature runs the other way, with automatic metrics \emph{understating}
damage relative to human judgement, and our own results are about the
\emph{difference} between arms under a fixed protocol rather than about whether
quantization is safe to deploy. We also report that leniency in extraction has
two-sided error, so nothing here argues for scoring more permissively as a
general remedy. The actionable content is that unreported measurement choices
should be reported, which raises the cost of a claim rather than lowering it.

Compute is reported in full: every run, its duration and its cost are recorded
in the released log, totalling roughly 35 GPU-hours on a single accelerator.

Every term here is also defined where it first appears in the text. This section is for
looking one up out of order, not the definition of record.

\begin{description}\itemsep2pt
  \item[arm] One precision setting of one model, for example Llama-3.1-8B at
    GPTQ-4bit. We compare five.
  \item[\texttt{bf16}] Brain floating point, 16 bits. The unquantized baseline every
    quantized setting is compared against.
  \item[GPTQ, AWQ] Two post-training quantization methods. Both produce a weight file
    ahead of time, which the serving engine then loads.
  \item[bitsandbytes NF4] A third method, 4-bit NormalFloat. Unlike GPTQ and AWQ it is
    applied \emph{in flight} when the model loads, rather than read from a prepared file,
    and it has no dedicated GPU kernels in our serving engine. Both facts matter: they are
    why the kernel remedy of \S\ref{sec:h8} does not apply to it.
  \item[kernel] The specialised GPU routine that performs one operation, here the
    quantized matrix multiplication. Different kernels compute the same mathematical
    result by different arithmetic routes, which is why the choice is observable.
  \item[Marlin, Machete] The two quantized matrix-multiply kernels our engine selects by
    default for 4-bit GPTQ and AWQ. Disabling them is the intervention in \S\ref{sec:h8}.
  \item[batch invariance] The property that a result does not depend on which other
    requests were processed alongside it. Our engine offers a mode intended to guarantee
    this; \S\ref{sec:h8} reports where that mode does not reach.
  \item[strict, permissive] The two answer-extraction rules whose disagreement this paper
    measures. \emph{Strict} is the evaluation harness's canonical regular expression, used
    verbatim. \emph{Permissive} is the harness's own flexible extractor, which accepts
    looser forms. Both are the harness's, not ours.
  \item[rung] One step of the extraction ladder in \S\ref{sec:ladder}. Five rungs run
    over the same stored text, so comparing them costs no additional generation.
  \item[interaction] Here, the difference between how much permissive extraction helps a
    quantized setting and how much it helps \texttt{bf16} on the same items. Zero means
    the extraction rule is a nuisance; non-zero means it changes the reported gap.
  \item[the gap, the reported difference] The number a paper reports as quantization
    degradation: the unquantized score minus the quantized score.
  \item[within a process, across processes] Whether two compared measurements were
    produced by the same running instance of the serving engine, or by two separate runs.
    \S\ref{sec:crossjob} shows this distinction is load-bearing.
\end{description}

\section{Annotation rubric}
\label{app:rubric}

% ⚠️ DELIBERATELY A STATED ABSENCE, 2026-09-05, not an oversight. The rubric requires the
% second annotator to draft and dry-run it jointly (task B5), which has not happened, so the
% human annotation (A11) and the judge validation gate (A12) have not run either. Saying so
% here is the honest option; an empty heading would imply the content exists somewhere.
%
% This is also what the Responsible NLP Checklist §D will force us to state, so it is better
% written once, here, and referenced.

Two annotators labelled 250 generations from the pilot run, stratified across 48 cells of
model, precision and task, as \emph{correct}, \emph{incorrect} or \emph{no answer}; 120
items were labelled by both. The rubric fixes a decision procedure in order: find the answer
the model presents as final, take the last commitment where there are several, compare by
value rather than formatting, and apply one written rule to each of nine edge cases, among
them truncation before versus after commitment, a correct number that appears only in the
reasoning, a hedged pair of answers, and a reference answer that looks wrong. It was fixed,
with a ten-item practice round completed, before any real label was recorded. The precision
condition and both automated verdicts were absent from the annotation file rather than
hidden in it, item order was shuffled independently per annotator with published seeds, and
no label was pre-filled.

Agreement on the overlap was 0.975 raw (95\% CI 0.929 to 0.991), Cohen's $\kappa$ 0.943
and Gwet's AC1 0.968. The three disagreements were resolved by looking up the rubric clause
that decides each item, never by deferring to either annotator, and one resolved to a label
neither had chosen. Against these labels, strict extraction agrees with the human verdict on
76.9\% of \texttt{bf16} items and 56.2\% of 4-bit items, with false-negative rates of
23.1\% and 41.1\%, while permissive extraction agrees on 85.6\% and 89.0\% with false
positives of at most 0.7\%. The rubric, seeds, labels and adjudication record are released.
