## Your four statistical questions, answered directly

**1. `bounds.py` — is the difference bound the widest? Yes, and your argument for it is the right one.** For arm $a$ with $n$ items, $k_a$ correct among committed and $m_a$ absent, $\theta_a \in [k_a/n, (k_a+m_a)/n]$. The unobserved outcomes under the two arms are distinct quantities with no constraint linking them, so the attainable set for $\theta_{alt} - \theta_{ref}$ is the interval difference $[\theta_{alt}^{L} - \theta_{ref}^{U}, \theta_{alt}^{U} - \theta_{ref}^{L}]$, exactly as implemented. Its width is $m_{alt}/n + m_{ref}/n$, the sum of the two arms' widths, which is the maximum possible; `alt_upper - ref_upper` would give a width of $|m_{alt} - m_{ref}|/n$ and understate it. This is the standard Horowitz-Manski worst-case bound for a difference of two partially identified means, it matches §1.3's *"for each arm independently, and propagate to the difference"* verbatim, and the extremes are jointly attainable so the bound is sharp under no assumptions. Analytic, not executed. Nothing to change.

**2. `transitions.py` and `decompose.stopseq_differential` — the `(2p-1)*scale` map is legitimate, not invented.** With $d = n_{12}+n_{21}$ and $p = n_{21}/d$, the net rate is $(n_{21}-n_{12})/n = (2p-1)\cdot d/n$ identically, so mapping a monotone interval on $p$ through $(2p-1)\cdot d/n$ is a valid interval for the net shift **conditional on $d$**. That is the same conditioning McNemar's exact test performs, which is what makes it the natural partner for the registered test. Two caveats worth a line in the docstring rather than a code change:

- It is a *conditional* interval, so it treats $d$ as fixed and does not carry the sampling variability of $d$ itself. The unconditional McNemar-type variance is $\big(n_{12}+n_{21}-(n_{12}-n_{21})^2/n\big)/n^2$. The conditional interval is defensible and arguably the one §1.2 implies, but the docstring should say "conditional on the discordant total" rather than leaving a reader to assume it is unconditional.
- Wilson excludes $p=0.5$ exactly when the **score** test rejects, not when the **exact** binomial test does, so the interval and the reported exact p-value can disagree near the boundary. If you want them to agree, use `method="beta"` (Clopper-Pearson), which is the exact-test partner. Your docstring's claim that this "is the interval that pairs with the exact McNemar test" is the one sentence that overreaches.

I verified by hand that the orientation is consistent: `net_pp` and $p$ both use the $n_{21}$ numerator and the map is increasing, so `ci_low` derives from the Wilson lower bound correctly and the point estimate lies inside the interval. The `n_discordant == 0` branch giving a symmetric $[-high, +high]$ from Wilson on 0-of-$n$ is a sensible choice and correctly avoids a zero-width interval.

**3. `interaction.py` — the bootstrap is right, subject to CRITICAL 1.** The per-item contribution identity holds exactly: since all four accuracies are over the same item set, the difference of differences equals $\frac1n\sum_i\big([\text{alt}_H-\text{alt}_L]-[\text{ref}_H-\text{ref}_L]\big)$, so `contrib.mean()` is the estimand and the runtime assert is a good guard on the property that makes the bootstrap valid. Resampling rows of `contrib` carries all four (arm, rung) observations of an item together, so the pairing across rungs and the clustering within items are both respected, and item is exactly the unit `(1|item)` designates. The percentile interval is appropriate because the statistic is a sample mean of bounded integers in $[-2,2]$, which is close to normal at these $n$ with negligible skew, so the first-order accuracy of the percentile method is not a limitation here; BCa would buy nothing. `n_boot=10_000` puts Monte Carlo error well below reporting precision, as claimed. The one thing wrong is *which items* end up in `shared`, which is CRITICAL 1.

**4. `holm_bonferroni` — the step-down is correct, including the part you asked about.** Thresholds are $\alpha/(m-k)$ for 0-based $k$, giving $\alpha/m$ for the smallest p-value and $\alpha/1$ for the largest, which is standard Holm. `still_rejecting` is set `False` at the first failure and never reset, so everything after a failure is retained regardless of its own p-value. Ties behave correctly in both directions. Recording `adjusted_alpha` for every tested effect is good for auditability, and keeping p-value-less effects in the rows while excluding them from `m` is the right call and correctly reasoned in the docstring.

## Q3 — the estimator question

**Your engineering is defensible, and I would make the same primary choice.** §2 asks for the interaction as a marginal effect in accuracy points with a 95% CI *and* as an odds ratio. The marginal path delivers the first exactly, assumption-free, with the interval derived from resampling the unit the registration names as the clustering. Making that primary and labelling the approximated OR is the honest ordering, and putting the fitter in both the `unit` string and the `context` is the right way to keep a reader from misreading a VB posterior interval as a frequentist CI.

**There is one fitter you missed, and it is a good fit: `statsmodels.discrete.conditional_models.ConditionalLogit`.** It is the Chamberlain conditional-likelihood estimator, which conditions the item intercepts out of the likelihood and gives **frequentist maximum-likelihood estimates of the within-item coefficients** with ordinary standard errors. It suits this design well, because `scoring` varies *within* item (the same generation is scored at both rungs), so the strict-versus-permissive contrast and its interaction with precision are identified within item. It is closer to what `(1|item)` is usually taken to mean than either VB or GEE, and it is genuinely in statsmodels rather than requiring `rpy2`.

Two caveats to state if you add it: it discards items with no outcome variation (all-correct or all-incorrect across the rows in a group), which on a floored or ceilinged cell can be most of them, so report the retained count; and coefficients of variables constant within item are not estimable, so `model` drops out, which is fine because it is absorbed rather than confounded. I would add it as a third `fitter` value rather than replacing anything, since three labelled estimators that agree is a stronger robustness statement than two.

For completeness on what genuinely is not available: `MixedLM` is Gaussian-only, `pymer4`/`rpy2` would import R, and `bambi`/`PyMC` is full MCMC. Your statement that statsmodels has no frequentist logistic mixed model is accurate.
