## MODERATE 1 — the registered magnitude is study-wide; the implementation is per-cell

`src/quantartifact/analysis/results.py:279-303`, `analysis/preregistration.md:1033`

**Why it is wrong.** §9h now reads *"the maximum `|d_i|` OBSERVED **anywhere in the study**, which under rung monotonicity is 1."* The implementation calls `observed_max_contribution(contrib)` with only the contributions of the cell being estimated. The two definitions coincide exactly while no monotonicity violation exists, because both return 1.

**How it fails.** Suppose one BBH subtask shows a genuine violation, `|d_i| = 2`. R26.1's registered rule then sets the magnitude to **2 for every cell in the study**, including ARC, and every zero-variance bound widens. The code instead computes ARC's magnitude from ARC's own all-zero contributions, floors it at 1, and reports `observed_max_abs_contribution: 1` with a 1.58 pp bound. The cells that most need widening are precisely the degenerate ones that cannot observe the violation themselves. So **the mechanism registered to propagate a violation does not propagate it**, and the divergence appears only in the contingency the mechanism was built for.

**Confirmed by:** reading, plus `grep` on `analysis/preregistration.md` line 1033 confirming the "anywhere in the study" wording, and on `interaction.py:233,242` confirming both call sites pass only the local `contrib`.

**Fix.** Compute the study-wide maximum once in `analyze_run.py` across every task's `contrib`, then pass it down: `interaction_marginal(..., max_abs_contribution=study_wide)`. That makes the reported number the registered one and makes a violation anywhere visible everywhere. If you would rather keep it per-cell, that is a change to §9h's wording and therefore the planner's, not yours — but it must be one or the other, because right now the file and the code say different things.

## Your four questions

**1. `observed_max_contribution`.** `(magnitude, n_violations)` is computed correctly: `n_violations = int((values > 1).sum())` counts exactly the `|d_i| > 1` cases that monotonicity forbids, and the empty-array raise is right and well-argued.

`max(1, ...)` is the right floor for the reason you gave and for one you did not: without it the all-zero case would make `zero_variance_bound` raise on `max_abs_contribution=0`, so the floor is load-bearing, not defensive. Its only problem is the label (MODERATE 2).

**On `int()` truncation: your concern is correctly identified, currently unreachable, and worth the one-word fix.** `contrib` is built from `int(...) - int(...)` arithmetic cast to float64, and integers up to 2^53 are exactly representable, so the values really are exactly `-2.0 … 2.0` and `int(values.max())` cannot see 1.9999999 from this caller. But `observed_max_contribution` is public and its docstring makes no representability promise, and `int()` truncates **toward zero**, which is the narrower-bound direction — the one §9h exists to prevent. `max(1, int(np.ceil(values.max())))` costs nothing and removes the hazard. NaN and inf both fail loud already (`int(nan)` raises `ValueError`, `int(inf)` raises `OverflowError`), which is the right behaviour.

**2. Magnitude consistency across a table: not inconsistent, and correct because they are different estimands.** They numerically agree at 1 today, but for different reasons, and the difference is load-bearing rather than incidental:

- A per-arm indicator's contribution is `±1` or `0` **by construction** — an item is discordant in one direction or not at all. Magnitude 1 is a fact about the arithmetic and can never change.
- The difference of differences is bounded by 1 **only under rung monotonicity**. If monotonicity were violated, the DoD magnitude would correctly rise to 2 while the per-arm magnitudes correctly stay at 1.

So the asymmetry is exactly right, and hardcoding the per-arm sites to 1 while deriving the DoD site is the correct treatment rather than an oversight. One presentational consequence worth a line: both paths now emit `interval_method` strings reading `max|contribution|=1`, so a reader sees identical justifications for structurally different guarantees. Naming the source in the string (`derived from rung monotonicity` versus `per-arm indicator, ±1 by construction`) would make the table self-explaining.

**3. Present-but-empty versus absent: your reasoning is right and your encoding defeats it.** See MODERATE 3. And to answer the second half directly: yes, something downstream treats an empty Holm list as a completed correction — the artifact does, because `h4_holm: []`, `h1_holm: []` and `h3_holm: []` are byte-identical while meaning three different things, and there is no field a consumer could read to tell them apart. You were right to be suspicious of this being the `score_template` shape; it is.

**4. Reintroduced for a third time: yes, but as a pattern rather than a single defect.** The instance is MODERATE 3. The pattern is worth naming on its own, because it has now produced three findings across three reviews and each looked different:

> **A guard in the library is not a guard if the caller can satisfy its precondition instead of its purpose.**

- `decompose(allow_excluded=True)` — the caller hands over a permission slip. Fixed by splitting the lists.
- `holm_bonferroni` raises on an empty family — the caller writes `if x else []`, so the raise never fires. Not fixed.

Both are the caller neutralising a guard by construction rather than by argument, and your own H3 comment already articulates the principle for the first one. The structural answer is to make the guard's outcome unrepresentable rather than avoidable: `holm_bonferroni` should return a family object whose `status` field the caller cannot fake, so there is no `[]` to substitute. That converts both instances into one fix, and it is the "make the bug impossible rather than merely absent" move.
